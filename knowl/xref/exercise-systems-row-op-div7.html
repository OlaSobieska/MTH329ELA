Exercise 20.

Multiply the first row by \(1/7\text{.}\)
\begin{equation*} \begin{abmatrix}{rrrr|r} 7 \amp -21 \amp 15 \amp 0 \amp 7 \\ 12 \amp - 5 \amp -4 \amp 1 \amp 8 \\ 11 \amp - 1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \amp -2 \end{abmatrix} \end{equation*}
🔗
Answer.
\begin{equation*} \begin{abmatrix}{rrrr|r} 7 \amp -21 \amp 15 \amp 0 \amp 7 \\ 12 \amp - 5 \amp -4 \amp 1 \amp 8 \\ 11 \amp - 1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \amp -2 \end{abmatrix} \xrightarrow{\frac{1}{7} R_1} \begin{abmatrix}{rrrr|r} 1 \amp -3 \amp \frac{15}{7} \amp 0 \amp 1 \\ 12 \amp -5 \amp -4 \amp 1 \amp 8 \\ 11 \amp -1 \amp -1 \amp 3 \amp -2 \end{abmatrix} \end{equation*}
🔗
Using variables \(x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4\text{,}\) the original first row represents the equation
\begin{equation*} 7 x_1 - 21 x_2 + 15 x_3 = 7 \text{.} \end{equation*}
Most of the coefficients and constant in this equation have a factor of \(7\) in common. Usually, in purely algebraic manipulations we would not divide this equation through by that common factor. But when we develop an algorithm for systematically simplifying systems of equations through matrix operations in Chapter 2, we will find that it is advantageous to perform operations to reduce the “leading” coefficient in some of the equations in the system to \(1\text{,}\) which is precisely what our chosen operation achieves:
\begin{equation*} 7 x_1 - 21 x_2 + 15 x_3 = 7 \quad \longrightarrow \quad x_1 - 3 x_2 + \tfrac{15}{7} x_3 = 1 \text{.} \end{equation*}
The coefficients/constant in this new equation correspond exactly to the new first row in our manipulated matrix above. (While we often try to avoid fractions in our matrices, there is no reason a matrix cannot contain fractional entries because there is no reason the coefficients in an equation cannot be fractions.)
